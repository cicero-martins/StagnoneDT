\section{Summary}

Work on the coupled three-dimensional model resumed on 5 August after a pause
since mid-June, due to the rewriting of the manuscripts related to GEE. As we
already had several runs done and the skeleton of the paper in place, the main
outcome of these days was to compile everything and finalise the first draft, in
LaTeX and formatted for \textit{Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science}.

The experimental design is a complete factorial in three factors, which needed
eight simulations. Two of them, those combining a mobile bed with no wave
coupling, required a change to the sediment configuration before they would run
to completion, and that change had to be applied to the other mobile-bed members
as well so that all four remain comparable. Those re-runs finished today, and
the results below are from the complete set.

The manuscript is provisionally titled \textbf{``Interacting effects of wave
coupling and bed mobility on Lagrangian transport in a shallow vegetated
lagoon''}, with the three of us as authors. I would be grateful for your
comments on both the framing and the content.

\section{What the study asks and what it found}

Coastal lagoon models are routinely validated against water level and then used
to describe transport, so I set the paper to ask whether the first licenses the
second. Eight configurations were run over the same nine days of July 2025,
covering the drifter deployments, from the same initial condition and forcing,
differing only in wave coupling, bed mobility, and whether seagrass resistance is
uniform or distributed from a satellite classification. Each was scored against
three tide gauges and against 35 GPS drifters over twelve deployments.

\textbf{Water level does not separate the configurations.} Anomaly RMSE differs
between them by at most 4.2~mm at any station, within the measurement
uncertainty, and no configuration is consistently best. Over the same
simulations Liu--Weisberg skill spans 0.469 to 0.705 and endpoint separation 336
to 625~m. A calibration on water level alone would have had no basis on which to
prefer any of them.

\textbf{Neither wave coupling nor bed mobility improves Lagrangian skill on its
own, and each is what makes the other work.} Applied singly they are the two
treatments that make the model worse. Applied together they give the ensemble
its two best members. The two worst and the two best configurations are built
from the same pair of treatments and differ only in whether both are switched
on.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Change in Liu--Weisberg skill & On a fixed bed & On a mobile bed \\
\midrule
From adding wave coupling & $-0.035$ to $-0.042$ & $+0.155$ to $+0.232$ \\
\midrule
                          & \textbf{Without waves} & \textbf{With waves} \\
From adding bed mobility  & $-0.071$ to $-0.075$ & $+0.119$ to $+0.199$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Bed mobility without waves makes every one of the 35 drifters worse, in both
roughness treatments, which is as consistent as a paired comparison can be. All
eight bootstrap intervals behind the table are clear of zero.

\textbf{Distributed seagrass roughness is a third-order refinement on this
evidence.} It changes skill by 0.009 or less in three of its four conditions,
and by 0.081 in the fourth, which is on a mobile bed with wave coupling. Its
effect on the wave field is nonetheless real and correctly ordered by canopy
density, reducing near-bed orbital velocity by 14.3\% over \emph{Posidonia}
against 3.3\% over exposed rock, so the parametrisation works. It simply does
not move surface transport unless the other two are present.

Splitting the trajectory error into a magnitude part and a direction part shows
the two moving together rather than independently, so the treatments appear to
act on the velocity field as a whole rather than on separable parts of the
error. And surface current speed exceeds bed speed by a factor of 1.85 to 2.20
at sub-metre mean depth, which is the quantitative case for solving in three
dimensions, consistent with De~Marchis et al.\ (2012) here. That ratio is set by
wave coupling rather than by bed mobility.

\section{Two questions for you}

\textbf{First, the title.} The one in the draft is my current preference, but
feel free to suggest changes. Here are other two alternatives that I think are defensible:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \emph{Interacting effects of wave coupling and bed mobility on
  Lagrangian transport in a shallow vegetated lagoon.} In the draft.
  Conservative and conventional in form.
  \item \emph{Wave coupling helps or hurts lagoon transport prediction depending
  on whether the bed can move.} A bit more bold, try to state the finding rather than the topic, and
  the conditional form claims what the eight configurations show.
  \item \emph{Transport validation of a shallow lagoon model: interacting
  effects of wave coupling and bed mobility.} States the method first, then the mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Second, and more consequential, whether to submit before obtaining a new
bathymetric survey.} The configurations that best reproduce observed transport
are the ones with a mobile bed, and its critical shear stress for erosion has
not been constrained against measured bathymetric change. We are using the old
bathymetry, so it is possible that a mobile bed helps because it moves the old
morphology towards the present one, in which case the improvement is a
compensation rather than a physical result. The manuscript states this openly,
and notes that the lagoon bed changes by up to 1.4~m over nine days in isolated
cells, which is not a plausible rate. If there is a realistic chance of
obtaining new bathymetry soon, maybe we should wait for it, and rerun. 
If not, we can submit valuing to declare a caveat that the improvement may be a compensation 
for an outdated bathymetry.
