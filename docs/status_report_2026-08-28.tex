\section{Two faults in the seagrass parametrisation}

My report of 8 August concluded that distributed seagrass roughness is a
third-order refinement, changing Lagrangian skill by 0.009 or less in three of
its four conditions. That conclusion was wrong, and the reason is now
established. Two separate faults meant the seagrass was never actually resisting
the flow, and once both are repaired the roughness term becomes the strongest
single effect in the ensemble rather than the weakest.

\textbf{The first fault was in the input file.} D-Flow FM matches the roughness
area file to the mesh with a tolerance of 1~cm, and our coordinates were written
with six decimals. That is about 11~cm at this latitude, so 94\% of the seagrass
links were silently discarded. The model was reporting a distributed roughness
field of which it had applied 5.5\%.

\textbf{The second fault is in the solver, and it is not ours.} The Baptist
formulation splits vegetation resistance into a bed roughness and a canopy
momentum sink, and the D-Flow FM technical reference states that the sink enters
the momentum equation. It does not. The routine computes it and the solver never
reads it. I established this by running two identical simulations whose only
difference was the canopy drag coefficient, zero against 0.8, giving a momentum
sink of \SI{0}{\per\metre} against \SI{4}{\per\metre} over 16359 links. The two
solutions are bit-identical across 48 output steps and 27901 cells. The
formulation had been delivering only half of itself, and the half it delivered
made the meadow smoother than bare sand.

Both faults look identical from the outside, which is why they survived so long.
The configuration file is accepted, the diagnostic echoes every parameter back
correctly, and the simulation runs to completion.

\section{The parametrisation now used, and how it was fixed}

Flexible Mesh carries a second, independent vegetation implementation that is
separate from the roughness machinery, and there the canopy term does reach the
momentum equation. I rebuilt the four vegetated members on it. Every parameter
comes from published measurement, and none was fitted to any observation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{6.4cm}}
\toprule
Parameter & Value & Source \\
\midrule
Shoot density & $500 \times 6$ leaves, times cover & Ciraolo et al.\ (2006), times the satellite classification \\
Leaf width & \SI{0.01}{\metre} & Ciraolo et al.\ (2006) \\
Canopy height & \SI{0.40}{\metre} & Prinos et al.\ (2010), submergence 0.32 to 0.50 \\
Leaf density & \SI{900}{\kilo\gram\per\cubic\metre} & Prinos et al.\ (2010), natural 800 to 1020 \\
Drag coefficient & 0.8 & rigid cylinder, not adjusted \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The canopy height deserves a note, because I had it wrong first. I took the leaf
length from your 2006 study, over \SI{1}{\metre} in nature, and used it
directly. What the model needs is the vertical extent the drag occupies, and for
a plant whose leaves lie over those differ, which is why your paper reports the
deflected thickness $k$ separately. Your measured $k/h$ sits mostly between 0.2
and 0.6, and field observation agrees that \emph{Posidonia} here rarely reaches
the surface. At \SI{0.40}{\metre} the modelled resistance lands within 3\% of
your Equation 22, evaluated at the model's own velocities and depths, with
nothing tuned against it.

Two checks that were not used to choose any parameter. Surface over
depth-averaged speed on the meadow is 1.60, where the roughest bed roughness we
can impose reaches 1.335, so the vertical signature is a canopy and not a rough
bed. And bed shear stress falls by a factor of 11, whereas an equivalent bed
roughness raises it: the meadow shelters the bed, which the previous
parametrisation had backwards.

The 35 drifters were held out of every one of these decisions.

Distributed roughness now helps in all four cells, not one, and the cell where
the August report said it acts is the cell where it acts least. Two independent
routes agree, the repaired roughness file and the native module, and they differ
from each other by 0.03 or less. Formula 154 is negative everywhere, which is
the signature of the inert sink rather than a third opinion.

\section{This bears directly on the bathymetry question}

In August I asked whether to wait for a new survey, on the reasoning that a
mobile bed might be helping because it moves an outdated morphology towards the
present one, making the improvement a compensation rather than a result. The
corrected ensemble gives evidence on exactly that mechanism, and it is not the
answer I expected.

With uniform roughness, waves alone reduce skill by 0.035 and a mobile bed alone
reduces it by 0.071, the latter making every one of the 35 drifters worse. Yet
together they raise it by 0.084. An additive model predicts $-0.106$, so the
interaction term is $+0.190$. With the seagrass correctly represented, that same
interaction term falls to $+0.028$. The synergy exists only when the meadow is
missing.

So the compensation is real, and part of what it compensated for was the absent
canopy rather than the old bathymetry. This does not remove the bathymetric
concern, but the earlier evidence for the interaction was resting partly on a
defect we have since fixed. Standard deviation of skill across the four cells is
0.057 for the uniform members and 0.016 for the vegetated ones: once the meadow
is right, transport skill is largely indifferent to whether waves and bed
mobility are on.

\section{Consequences and what I propose}

Sections 4.3 and 5 have to be rewritten, and the central claim changes from an
interaction between waves and bed mobility to something I think is stronger: a
physically grounded canopy is what the transport prediction needs, and omitting
it manufactures a synergy between two other processes.

I would value your view on two points. Whether to present the inert momentum
sink as a finding in the paper, with the bit-identical test as evidence, or to
report it to Deltares separately and keep the paper on the physics. And whether
the title should now foreground the seagrass.

One limitation to record. Your Equation 22 makes the friction factor depend on
velocity, and Flexible Mesh offers that only in its depth-averaged solver, while
ours is three-dimensional. It does carry a mechanical bending model, which I
verified is genuinely dynamic, but its amplitude over our velocity range is
about 4\%, against the factor of twelve your measurements span between 2 and
\SI{30}{\centi\metre\per\second}.
