\section{Summary}

Work on the coupled three-dimensional model resumed on 5 August after a pause
since mid-June, due to the rewriting of the manuscripts related to GEE. 
As we have several runs already done, the main outcome of the past three days is that the first
manuscript now has a complete draft, in LaTeX, formatted for
\textit{Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science} and within all of that journal's
limits. A numerical problem that had cost us two of the eight experiments was
also traced and fixed today, and the corresponding re-runs are in progress
(Section~3).

The manuscript is provisionally titled \textbf{``Transport validation of a
wave-coupled lagoon model: interacting effects of seagrass roughness and bed
mobility''}, with the three of us as authors. I would be grateful for your
comments on both the framing and the content.

\section{What the study asks and what it found}

Considering that coastal lagoon models are routinely validated against water level and then used
to describe transport, I set the paper to ask whether the first licenses the second. Six
model configurations were run over the same nine days of July 2025, during the drifters deployments,
from the same initial condition and forcing, differing only in wave coupling, bed
mobility, and whether seagrass resistance is uniform or distributed from a
satellite classification. Each was scored against three tide gauges and against
35 GPS surface drifters released over twelve deployments.

\textbf{Water level does not separate the configurations.} In the results, WL anomaly RMSE differs
by six millimetres or less between the runs at any station, which is within the
measurement uncertainty, and no configuration is consistently best across the
three stations. Over the same simulations, using Liu--Weisberg skill score, we obtained
values spanning from 0.505 to 0.655 and endpoint separation spans 334 to 661~m. 
A calibration conducted on water level alone would have had no basis on which to prefer any of them.

\textbf{No treatment improves Lagrangian skill on its own.} Distributed seagrass
roughness on a fixed bed changes skill by 0.009 or less, bed mobility under
uniform roughness by 0.019, and wave coupling reduces it by 0.035 to 0.042.
Applied together, distributed roughness and bed mobility raise skill by 0.131 to
0.149, improving 32 of the 35 drifters, and only that combination reproduces the
observed transport rate.

\textbf{Splitting the trajectory error into magnitude and direction shows what
each treatment corrects}, which is the most recent result and the one I consider
the paper's contribution.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Configuration & Path length & Net displacement & Heading error \\
              & (sim/obs)   & (sim/obs)        & (deg) \\
\midrule
Four fixed-bed members & 0.77--0.80 & 0.78--0.82 & 15.8--16.2 \\
Mobile bed, uniform roughness & 0.94 & 0.95 & 12.5 \\
Mobile bed, distributed roughness & 0.98 & 0.99 & \textbf{8.4} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The four fixed-bed members coincide in all three measures. Bed mobility acts on
magnitude, restoring the distance travelled. Distributed roughness acts on
direction, and does so only once the bed can move: on a fixed bed it leaves the
heading error unchanged to the first decimal. This is reported as a narrowing
rather than a complete mechanism, since reconstructing endpoint separation from
a mean displacement ratio and a mean heading still underpredicts the two
mobile-bed members by a factor of 1.4 to 1.9.

Until now, I could not find a simpler explanation: the spatial pattern of bed
change is indistinguishable between the two mobile-bed members, canopy drag does
not suppress erosion beneath the meadows, and the successful configuration does
not carry a stronger mean current field.

Two further results support the modelling choices. The canopy parametrisation
reduces mean wave orbital velocity at the bed by 14.3\% over \emph{Posidonia},
13.7\% over \emph{Cymodocea}, 11.3\% over sand and 3.3\% over exposed rock, an
ordering that follows canopy density. And surface speed exceeds bed speed by a
factor of 1.9 to 4.5 at sub-metre mean depth, consistent with De~Marchis et
al.\ (2012) here and the quantitative case for solving in three dimensions.

\section{An item that closed while this was being written}

The design is a factorial in three factors, and until today only six of its
eight cells had been obtained. The two missing cells combine a mobile bed with
no wave coupling, and both aborted on the model's velocity cap, at 26.96 and
43.42~\si{\metre\per\second} against a limit of 25. They did so
deterministically: two independent attempts stopped at the same simulated
instant with velocities identical to six decimal places. The manuscript as it
stands reports this as a property of the system, under a heading stating that a
mobile bed does not stabilise without waves.

That reading was wrong, and I am glad the check was made before submission. The
instability turned out to be offshore and diffuse rather than a defective cell:
of the 150 fastest cells before the abort, none was inside the lagoon, none was
above the datum, and their median depth was 34~m.

The cause is the coupling between suspended sediment and density. The morphology
settings had suspended sediment feeding the density field, and therefore the
baroclinic pressure gradient, which is the only route by which sediment can move
momentum in a deep cell holding no erodible material. It also explains an
earlier fix that failed: confining erodible thickness to depths above 20~m
changed nothing, because that bounds erosion and not the suspended load arriving
by advection. With the coupling disabled the run passed the abort instant
without incident and is now at 65\% of the nine days, with a time step almost
double what it had before.

Three re-runs are under way so that all four mobile-bed members share the
setting, and should finish overnight. The design then closes at eight cells, and
the effect of wave coupling becomes measurable on a mobile bed, which the
manuscript currently states cannot be done. Sections 3.6, 4.3, 5 and 6 will be
revised, and the subsection asserting that a mobile bed does not stabilise
without waves will be removed rather than reworded. I do not expect the results
in Section~2 to move, since they rest on members that were never affected, but
they will be recomputed and checked.

\section{Manuscript status and what I would ask of you}

The draft is complete across all six sections, with eight figures and three
tables, 6200 words of body text against a limit of 8000, and 24 references
verified against the source PDFs. Highlights and a cover letter are drafted.
Outstanding are a graphical abstract, which \textit{ECSS} requires.

I would welcome your reading on three points in particular:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0.25em]
  \item Whether the framing holds. The paper claims a validation-strategy
  result, trying not to sit just as an study case of the Stagnone, 
  since the journal does not accept manuscripts of mainly local interest. Did you agree?
  \item Whether reporting the bed-mobility result is wise given that its
  critical shear stress for erosion has not been constrained against a repeated
  bathymetric survey. We are using the old bathymetry we have, maybe the results
  are better for mobile beds because it changes the morphology near the nowadays morphology, 
  and if we try again with updated data, the results could be completely different.
  The manuscript states this limitation openly, and the lagoon bed
  changes by up to 1.0~m over nine days in isolated cells, which is not a
  plausible rate. There's a chance that we obtain the new bathymetry soon, 
  and if so, we should wait to rerun the simulations with it before submission. 
  \item The CRediT roles I have assigned, which follow those of the reservoirs
  manuscripts.
\end{enumerate}

The full draft, figures and analysis code are in the project repository, and I
can send the compiled PDF on request.
