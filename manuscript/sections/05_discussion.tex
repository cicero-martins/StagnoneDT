\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Where water level can and cannot separate configurations}

The eight members differ in wave coupling, bed mobility and the representation
of the seagrass canopy, and at the two stations nearest the inlets they produce
water level records that are almost the same. Anomaly RMSE varies by 2.1~mm
between members at BocaSud and by 9.2~mm at BocaNord, in neither case with an
ordering that survives from one station to the other.

At AltaVilaEst it varies by 36.2~mm and the ordering is unambiguous. The four
vegetated members give 0.015 to 0.027~m and the four bare ones 0.045 to
0.051~m, with correlations of 0.94 to 0.98 against 0.78 to 0.82. That station
is the innermost of the three, in the shallow northern basin where the meadow is
densest, and it is the only one whose water level is set more by what happens
inside the lagoon than by the boundary.

Two conclusions follow, and they pull in different directions. The first is the
familiar one. A calibration exercise conducted on the outer stations alone would
have no basis on which to prefer any of these configurations, and would
plausibly select whichever member minimised a station-averaged RMSE by a margin
smaller than the measurement uncertainty. Over the same simulations
Liu--Weisberg skill spans 0.469 to 0.735 and endpoint separation spans 345 to
618~m. Reporting coastal model skill through water level statistics alone can
certify transport behaviour that was never examined.

The second is a qualification of it. Water level is not inherently blind to
these processes, it is blind where it is measured. The station inside the
vegetated basin separates the ensemble on exactly the factor the drifters
identify, and it does so on the same side. Gauge placement, rather than the
choice of water level as a variable, is what decided the outcome here. In a
micro-tidal basin with two open connections the level near an inlet is set
largely by the offshore forcing and the basin's capacity to fill and drain,
both of which the eight members share.

\subsection{The seagrass canopy is the first-order control on transport}

The central result is that a physically grounded canopy is what this basin's
transport prediction needs, and that it is the largest of the three treatments
examined by a wide margin.

Introducing the canopy raises Liu--Weisberg skill by 0.087 to 0.256 depending on
the configuration it is introduced into, with every bootstrap interval clear of
zero and between 26 and 32 of the 35 drifters improving in each of the four
contrasts. No other factor in the design produces four contrasts that share a
sign. Wave coupling ranges from $-0.042$ to $+0.155$ and bed mobility from
$-0.071$ to $+0.119$, in both cases changing sign according to the state of the
other two factors.

The effect is also a floor rather than a tuning. None of the canopy parameters
was fitted to any observation used to score it. Shoot density and leaf width
come from flume measurements of \emph{Posidonia oceanica}
\citep{CiraoloFerreri2006}, scaled by the fractional cover of the satellite
classification. Canopy height and leaf density come from published
measurements of the same species \citep{Prinos2010}. The drag coefficient is
the standard rigid-cylinder value. The drifters were held out of every one of
those choices and confirm the result afterwards.

\subsection{What the canopy acts on}

Splitting the trajectory error into a magnitude part and a direction part shows
that the canopy acts on both at once (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}). For each drifter
the simulated path length, the simulated net displacement and the mean angular
difference between simulated and observed step directions were computed over the
scored window.

The four bare members span 0.73 to 0.84 of the observed path length and 11.3 to
19.8$^\circ$ of heading error. The four vegetated members collapse into a much
narrower band, 0.88 to 0.94 and 8.3 to 9.0$^\circ$, and they do so regardless of
their wave and bed treatment. Every treatment that improves the trajectory
improves both components together and every treatment that degrades it degrades
both, which is what would be expected if they act on the velocity field as a
whole rather than on separable components of the error.

The mechanism the model offers is vertical. Section~\ref{sec:res_3d} shows the
canopy reducing bed-layer speed by roughly a factor of four while reducing
surface speed by much less, taking the surface-to-bed ratio from about 2 to
about 5. A surface drifter samples the top of that profile. Removing momentum
from the lower layers redistributes it in a way that changes where the surface
layer goes, and that is visible in the heading error as much as in the distance
travelled.

\subsection{Waves and bed mobility interact only where the canopy is absent}
\label{sec:disc_synergy}

In the bare arm, wave coupling and bed mobility each reduce Lagrangian skill
when applied alone, by 0.035 and 0.071, the latter degrading all 35 drifters.
Applied together they raise it by 0.084. An additive model predicts $-0.105$, so
the interaction term is $+0.189$, and the two worst and the second-best members
of the ensemble are built from the same pair of treatments differing only in
whether both are switched on. Read on its own, that half of the design supports
a strong statement about process coupling.

It does not survive the canopy. In the vegetated arm the same interaction term
is $+0.028$, the wave contrast on a mobile bed is $-0.014$ with an interval
crossing zero, and the bed contrast with waves is $+0.018$, also crossing zero.
The standard deviation of skill across the four cells is 0.057 in the bare arm
and 0.016 in the vegetated one.

A compensation is the reading the rest of the evidence supports. Without a
canopy the modelled bed is too smooth and the vertical profile too uniform, and
a wave field acting on a bed that can respond reproduces by another route part
of the flow structure the meadow would have produced. With the meadow in place
there is nothing left for that combination to reproduce. This is an inference
from the design rather than a demonstration of the mechanism, but it is
consistent with the vertical evidence, where the canopy moves the
surface-to-bed ratio by 2.6 to 3.1 and wave coupling moves it by 0.2 to 0.8.

The practical consequence is for how such an ensemble is read. Canopy drag is
often left out of lagoon and estuary models, or folded into a bed roughness
coefficient, and an ensemble built that way can still return a well-defined and
statistically clean interaction between the processes it does carry. Here that
interaction is confined to the four configurations without a canopy, so it
describes those configurations rather than the basin. Nothing internal to the
bare half of the design shows this, and it became visible only because the
canopy was varied as a factor in its own right and scored against an
observation type no model decision had used.

\subsection{The bed-mobility caveat, revisited}

In an earlier reading of this ensemble we asked whether the improvement from a
mobile bed might be a compensation for an outdated bathymetry, on the reasoning
that an evolving bed would be moving an old morphology towards the present one.
Over nine days the modelled lagoon bed changes by up to 1.4~m in isolated cells
against a median of 0.05~m, in a basin whose mean depth is 0.95~m, which is not
a physically plausible rate for this system.

The corrected ensemble bears on that question directly. The improvement from a
mobile bed exists only in the bare arm, where it is $+0.119$ with waves, and
falls to $+0.018$ with an interval crossing zero once the canopy is present. So
part of what bed mobility was compensating for was the absent canopy rather than
the old bathymetry.

This does not remove the bathymetric concern. It does mean that the evidence
which motivated it was resting partly on a defect that has since been repaired,
and that the configuration reproducing observed transport no longer depends on
morphodynamics whose critical shear stress has not been constrained by survey.
The best-performing member of the ensemble carries a fixed bed. A repeated
bathymetric survey remains the observation that would settle the question, and
satellite-derived bathymetry over repeated epochs is the most tractable route
currently available, but the transport result no longer waits on it.

\subsection{Vertical structure}

Surface speed exceeds bed speed by a factor between 4.4 and 5.3 in the lagoon
interior when the canopy is represented, and between 1.8 and 2.1 when it is not,
in a basin whose mean depth is below one metre. A depth-averaged representation
would assign a single velocity to the layer a surface drifter samples and the
layer that governs bed shear stress, and in the vegetated configuration those
differ by a factor of five.

This is consistent with what \citet{DeMarchis2012} found from field measurements
and three-dimensional modelling in the same lagoon, and it supplies a
quantitative basis for the choice of a three-dimensional solver here. It also
sharpens that basis. The vertical structure is set by the treatment that also
dominates transport, so a depth-averaged model of this system would not simply
lose resolution in the vertical, it would lose the representation of the process
that governs where material goes.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limits}

Three limitations bear on how far these results extend.

\textbf{The canopy does not reach the wave model.} In this coupling the flow
model passes a bed roughness field to the wave model, and the canopy enters the
momentum equation of the flow rather than that roughness field. Mean orbital
velocity at the bed therefore changes by less than 2.5\% when the canopy is
introduced, and not in an order that follows canopy density
(Section~\ref{sec:res_classes}). Seagrass is known to attenuate waves as well as
currents, so the wave field in these simulations is that of a bare bed and the
wave contrasts reported here are conditional on that. Representing canopy
dissipation inside the spectral model is the natural next step and would be
expected to change the wave contrasts rather than the canopy ones, the latter
being measured on the flow.

\textbf{Reconfiguration is mechanical, and sensitive to a tabulated parameter.}
The canopy in these runs bends under load rather than holding a fixed height,
and that response is genuinely dynamic. A controlled pair differing only in
leaf density, 900 against 1020~\si{\kilo\gram\per\cubic\metre}, gives a velocity
ratio of 1.005 in the slowest quintile of the record, 0.908 in the fastest and
0.807 in the fastest 5\%. A fixed shortening of the canopy would give a constant
ratio, so the bending responds to load rather than to configuration alone.

Two things follow. A 13\% change in leaf density moves the velocity field by
20\% at the upper end of the range, which is a strong sensitivity to a value
taken from a published table rather than measured in this meadow. And the
bending is mechanical, where the flume measurements of
\citet{CiraoloFerreri2006} describe reconfiguration empirically, through a
friction factor that varies by a factor of twelve between 2 and
30~\si{\centi\metre\per\second}. That empirical formulation is available in the
model only in its depth-averaged solver, and the model used here is
three-dimensional, so the two cannot be compared directly and the agreement
between them was not tested.

\textbf{The window is short and the vegetated members are restart segments.}
The scored interval is two days for water level and thirty-four hours for the
drifter record, within a single summer season. The vegetated members are
three-day restart segments while the bare members are continuous nine-day runs,
and although a control run confirms that the restart contributes nothing to the
water level result (Section~\ref{sec:res_wl}), seasonal behaviour, winter storm
conditions and the interaction of the canopy with a mobile bed over longer
periods are outside what this design can address.
