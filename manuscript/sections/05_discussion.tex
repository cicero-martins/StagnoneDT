\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{A canopy is not a rough bed, and this basin can tell}

The question this study set out to answer was which modelled processes control
transport in a shallow vegetated lagoon. The answer is the seagrass canopy, by a
margin that leaves the other two treatments second order, and the mechanism the
model offers is vertical rather than frictional.

Introducing the canopy removes momentum from the lower part of the water column
without removing a proportionate amount from the upper part, so the ratio of
surface to bed speed roughly doubles. That is the signature of a canopy shear
layer rather than of a rougher bed, which acts through a logarithmic profile
whose surface-to-bed ratio is bounded well below the value observed here
\citep{Nepf2012}. The bed shear stress confirms the distinction by its sign, the
canopy sheltering the bed where an equivalent bed roughness raises the stress
over the same cells. A surface drifter samples the top of that profile, so a
treatment that reshapes the profile relocates the drifter even where it changes
the depth-averaged transport little.

This is what separates the present result from earlier models of the same basin.
\citet{DeMarchis2012} represented the two seagrass species through Nikuradse
roughness heights in a three-dimensional model, and \citet{Ingrassia2024}
obtained a best-fit uniform Gauckler--Strickler coefficient for the vegetated
lagoon in a depth-averaged one. Both treat the meadow as a property of the bed.
Doing so reproduces the drag but not the profile, and it is the profile that the
Lagrangian observations respond to. The canopy parameters used here were taken
from published measurement of the two species and were not adjusted against any
observation, so the improvement is a consequence of where the drag is applied
rather than of how it was tuned.

\subsection{Why one gauge saw this and two did not}

Water level separated the configurations at AltaVilaEst and at neither of the
gauges near the inlets. The asymmetry is structural. In a micro-tidal basin with
two open connections the level near an inlet is set largely by the offshore
forcing and by the basin's capacity to fill and drain, both of which every
configuration shares. The level in the interior is set by what the water does on
its way there, which is what the canopy changes.

The familiar reading of this is that water level constrains transport weakly and
that Lagrangian observations are a distinct constraint rather than a
confirmation \citep{Liu2011, Revelard2021}. That reading survives, but it needs a
qualification the present result makes available. Water level is not inherently
insensitive to the canopy. It is insensitive where it is usually measured, and
the one gauge sited inside the vegetated basin identified the same factor as the
drifters and ranked the configurations the same way. For a basin of this type
the informative question is not whether to validate on water level but where to
put the instrument, and the interior of the vegetated compartment is a
substantially more discriminating location than the inlet.

\subsection{What an ensemble without a canopy reports}
\label{sec:disc_synergy}

Read on its own, the bare half of this design supports a strong statement about
process coupling. Wave coupling and bed mobility each reduce Lagrangian skill
when applied alone and raise it when applied together, with a large interaction
term, tight bootstrap intervals and a mechanism available in the literature,
since wave orbital motion sets the bed stress that mobilises sediment and
wave--tide coupling is known to govern the sediment dynamics of shallow
micro-tidal basins \citep{Carniello2011}.

That statement does not survive the canopy. In the vegetated half the same
interaction term falls by most of its value and both contrasts lose their
significance, and the spread of skill across the four configurations contracts
by a factor of three.

The reading the rest of the evidence supports is compensation. Without a canopy
the modelled profile is too uniform, and a wave field acting on a bed that can
respond reproduces by another route part of the structure the meadow would have
produced. With the meadow present there is nothing left for that combination to
reproduce. This is an inference from the design rather than a demonstration of
the mechanism, and it is consistent with the vertical evidence, where the canopy
moves the surface-to-bed ratio by an order of magnitude more than wave coupling
does.

The consequence is for how such an ensemble is read rather than for this basin
alone. Canopy drag is commonly left out of lagoon and estuary models or folded
into a bed roughness coefficient, and an ensemble built that way can still
return a well-defined and statistically clean interaction between the processes
it does carry. Here that interaction is confined to the configurations without a
canopy, so it describes those configurations rather than the system. Nothing
internal to the bare half of the design distinguishes the two readings. What
distinguished them was varying the canopy as a factor in its own right and
scoring the result against an observation type that no model decision had used.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limits}

\textbf{The canopy does not reach the wave model.} The coupling passes a bed
roughness field to the spectral model, and the canopy enters the momentum
equation of the flow rather than that field, so mean orbital velocity at the bed
changes by under 2.5\% when the canopy is introduced and not in an order that
follows canopy density. Seagrass meadows do attenuate waves, in this species and
at this scale \citep{Infantes2012, Prinos2010}, and shallow restricted
Mediterranean lagoons carry wave fields that respond to bed conditions
\citep{Morote2025}. The wave field in these simulations is therefore that of a
bare bed, and the wave contrasts reported here are conditional on that.
Representing canopy dissipation inside the spectral model is the natural next
step. It would be expected to change the wave contrasts rather than the canopy
ones, which are measured on the flow.

\textbf{Reconfiguration is mechanical, and sensitive to a tabulated parameter.}
The canopy bends under load rather than holding a fixed height, and the response
is genuinely dynamic, the velocity ratio between two runs differing only in leaf
stiffness moving from 1.005 in the slowest quintile of the record to 0.807 in
the fastest 5\%. A fixed shortening would give a constant ratio. Two things
follow. A 13\% change in leaf density moves the velocity field by 20\% at the
upper end of the range, which is a strong sensitivity to a value taken from a
published table rather than measured in this meadow. And the bending is
mechanical, where the flume measurements of \citet{CiraoloFerreri2006} describe
reconfiguration empirically through a friction factor that varies by a factor of
twelve over the velocity range they tested. That empirical formulation is
available in the model only in its depth-averaged solver, so the two could not
be compared directly and the agreement between them was not tested.

\textbf{The morphodynamic factor is not calibrated.} The critical shear stress
for erosion has not been constrained against a repeated bathymetric survey, and
over nine days the modelled bed changes by up to 1.4~m in isolated cells against
a median of 0.05~m in a basin of 0.95~m mean depth, which is not a plausible
rate for this system. The factor is a sensitivity test rather than a prediction.
It matters less to the conclusions than it would have before, because the
configuration reproducing observed transport no longer depends on it. The
best-performing configuration of the eight carries a fixed bed.

\textbf{The window is short and the vegetated configurations are restart
segments.} Two days of water level and thirty-four hours of drifter record,
within one summer season. A control run establishes that the restart accounts
for none of the water level result (Section~\ref{sec:res_wl}), but seasonal
behaviour, storm conditions and the interaction of a canopy with a mobile bed
over longer periods are outside what this design addresses.

\textbf{One of the two species is loosely constrained.} The
\emph{C.\ nodosa} parameters come from a flume study of plants collected
elsewhere, and no measurement of that species in this lagoon was available. It
carries about 1.5\% of the canopy frontal area in the model, which bounds the
consequence, but the \emph{P.\ oceanica} column is the one doing the work.
