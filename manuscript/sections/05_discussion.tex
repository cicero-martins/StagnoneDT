\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Water level cannot separate these configurations}

The eight members differ in wave coupling, bed mobility and the spatial
treatment of vegetation resistance, and they produce water level records that
are almost the same. Anomaly RMSE varies by at most 4.2~mm between members at
any station, and no member is consistently best across the three stations. Over
the same simulations Liu--Weisberg skill spans 0.469 to 0.705 and endpoint
separation spans 336 to 625~m.

A calibration exercise conducted on water level alone would therefore have no
basis on which to prefer any of these configurations, and would plausibly
select whichever member happened to minimise a station-averaged RMSE by a
margin smaller than the measurement uncertainty. This is the case for treating
Lagrangian observations as a distinct constraint rather than as a confirmation
of a model already tuned to tide gauges. It also suggests that the frequent
practice of reporting coastal model skill through water level statistics alone
may certify transport behaviour that was never examined.

The reason is structural rather than incidental. Water level in a micro-tidal
basin with two open connections is set largely by the offshore forcing and the
basin's capacity to fill and drain, both of which the eight members share.
Transport depends on the velocity field, which they do not.

\subsection{Wave coupling and bed mobility are not separable}

The central result is that neither wave coupling nor bed mobility improves
Lagrangian prediction on its own, and that each is what makes the other work.

Applied singly they are the two treatments that make the model worse. Wave
coupling on a fixed bed costs 0.035 to 0.042 in skill. Bed mobility without
waves costs 0.071 to 0.075, and does so for every drifter in the set, 35 of 35
in both roughness treatments, which is as consistent as a paired comparison can
be. Applied together they gain 0.119 to 0.232 depending on which is treated as
the increment. The two worst and the two best members of the ensemble are built
from the same pair of treatments and differ only in whether both are switched
on.

The mechanism is available in the model even if this design cannot isolate it.
Wave orbital motion sets the bed shear stress that mobilises sediment, so a
mobile bed without waves erodes under current stress alone and evolves towards a
bathymetry the wave field never shaped. The reverse is equally intelligible: a
wave field acting on a bed that cannot respond imposes stress with no
morphological consequence, so its contribution to the momentum balance is
carried entirely by the flow, and in this basin over this window that
contribution is slightly negative. Neither half of the argument establishes the
mechanism, and we report the interaction as an empirical result of the design.

The practical consequence is that neither treatment can be assessed on its own.
A wave contribution measured on a fixed bed and a bed contribution measured
without waves each describe a corner of the design rather than the treatment,
and in this basin the two corners carry opposite signs to the ones the full
design gives. Where two model components are expected to interact, the
conditions under which an effect was measured belong with the effect.

\subsection{What the interaction acts on}

Splitting the trajectory error into a magnitude part and a direction part
locates the interaction (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}). For each drifter the
simulated path length, the simulated net displacement and the mean angular
difference between simulated and observed step directions were computed over the
scored window.

The ordering is monotonic. The no-wave
mobile-bed members are worst on both, at 0.73 of the observed path length and
19.8$^\circ$ of heading error. The four fixed-bed members sit between them and
the rest, at 0.77 to 0.80 and 15.8 to 16.2$^\circ$, and are indistinguishable
from each other. Wave coupling with a mobile bed reaches 0.84 and 11.3$^\circ$,
and adding distributed roughness reaches 0.88 and 8.5$^\circ$.

The two parts of the error move together. Every treatment that improves the
trajectory improves both at once, and every treatment that degrades it degrades
both. That is what would be expected if they act on the velocity
field as a whole rather than on separable components of the error, and it means
no treatment here can be described as correcting distance or direction
specifically.

Distributed roughness is a third-order refinement. It changes skill by 0.009 or
less in three of its four conditions and by 0.081 in the fourth, which is on a
mobile bed with wave coupling. Its effect on the wave field is nonetheless real
and correctly ordered: mean orbital velocity at the bed falls by 14.3\% over
\emph{Posidonia}, 13.7\% over \emph{Cymodocea}, 11.3\% over sand and 3.3\% over
the exposed rock near the inlets, following canopy density. The canopy
parametrisation does to the waves and to the near-bed layer what a canopy
should. What it does not do, unless the bed can also respond, is change surface
transport.

\subsection{The bed-mobility caveat}

The configuration that reproduces observed transport depends on morphodynamics
whose critical shear stress for erosion has not been constrained by a repeated
bathymetric survey. Over nine days the lagoon bed changes by up to 1.4~m in
isolated cells, against a median of 0.05~m, in a basin whose mean depth is
0.95~m. That rate of bed change is not physically
plausible rate for this system.

Two readings remain open. Bed mobility may be standing in for a deficiency in
the initial bathymetry, which dates from an earlier survey, in which case the
improvement is a compensation: an evolving bed would be moving an outdated
morphology towards the present one, and the gain would not survive a corrected
bed. Or the model may genuinely require an evolving bed to reproduce transport
in a system where the seagrass canopy and the sediment surface adjust to one
another, in which case the calibration problem is real but the physics is too.

The two readings are distinguishable, and the observation that would separate
them is a bathymetric survey rather than a modelling choice. Satellite-derived
bathymetry over repeated epochs is the most tractable route currently available.
Until then the interaction should be read as established in the model and
unverified against the bed.

\subsection{Vertical structure}

Surface speed exceeds bed speed by a factor between 1.85 and 2.20 in the lagoon
interior, in a basin whose mean depth is below one metre. A depth-averaged
representation would assign a single velocity to the layer a surface drifter
samples and the layer that governs bed shear stress, which differ by a factor of
two.

The ratio is set by wave coupling. The four members without waves lie between
1.85 and 1.87 whatever their bed treatment, and the four with waves between 2.05
and 2.20, with bed mobility contributing an order of magnitude less than the
wave field. Wave-induced mixing and streaming acting on the near-bed layer is
the expected route, and the sign and magnitude are consistent with it.

This is consistent with what \citet{DeMarchis2012} found from field measurements
and three-dimensional modelling in the same lagoon, and it supplies a
quantitative basis for the choice of a three-dimensional solver here. It also
shows that the vertical structure responds to the same model choice that
dominates transport, so the two are not independent properties of the basin.
