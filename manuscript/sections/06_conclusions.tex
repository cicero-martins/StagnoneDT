\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Six configurations of a coupled three-dimensional model of the Stagnone di
Marsala, differing in wave coupling, bed mobility and the spatial treatment of
seagrass resistance, produce water level records that cannot be told apart.
Anomaly RMSE differs by six millimetres or less between them at any station,
and no configuration is consistently best across the three stations. The same
six differ by 30\% in Liu--Weisberg skill and by a factor of two in endpoint
separation. Water level skill, on its own, certifies a model's capacity to fill
and drain a basin and says little about where the water inside it goes.

No treatment applied alone improves Lagrangian skill. Distributed seagrass
roughness on a fixed bed changes it by 0.009 or less, bed mobility under
uniform roughness by 0.019, and wave coupling reduces it by 0.035 to 0.042.
Each of these is one axis of a design in which two of the eight cells could not
be obtained, since a mobile bed without wave coupling aborts on the velocity
cap while the wave-coupled members carrying the same sediment settings complete.
The wave contribution is therefore measured on a fixed bed only.

Distributed roughness and bed mobility applied together raise skill by 0.13 to
0.15, improving 32 of the 35 drifters, and only that combination reproduces the
observed transport rate, at 0.97 of the observed path length against 0.78 to
0.93 for the other five. The two treatments interact rather than contribute
additively.

Separating the trajectory error into a magnitude part and a direction part
identifies what each treatment corrects. The four fixed-bed members coincide,
at 0.77 to 0.80 of the observed path length and a mean heading error of 15.8 to
16.2$^\circ$. Bed mobility acts on magnitude, raising path length to 0.94 and
net displacement to 0.95. Distributed roughness acts on direction, cutting the
heading error to 8.4$^\circ$, and it does nothing to heading at all when the bed
is held fixed. This narrows the interaction without fully accounting for it,
since reconstructing endpoint separation from a mean displacement ratio and a
mean heading underpredicts the two mobile-bed members by a factor of 1.4 to 1.9.
Three simpler explanations were tested and do not hold. The interaction is not
the spatial pattern of bed change, which is indistinguishable between the two
mobile-bed configurations, nor suppression of erosion beneath the canopy, which
runs in the opposite direction in both, nor a stronger mean current field, since
the successful configuration has a lower lagoon-mean surface speed than a
configuration whose particles travel 20\% too slowly.

The configuration reproducing observed transport carries an unconstrained
parameter. Its critical shear stress for erosion has not been fixed against a
repeated bathymetric survey, and the lagoon bed changes by up to 1.0~m over nine
days in isolated cells against a median of 0.05~m. Whether the improvement
reflects bed evolution or compensation for a deficient initial bathymetry cannot
be decided without observational constraint on the bed, and satellite-derived
bathymetry over repeated epochs is the most tractable route to it.

Surface current speed exceeds bed speed by a factor of 1.9 to 4.5 in the lagoon
interior, in a basin of sub-metre mean depth. That ratio itself depends on
whether the bed is allowed to move, rising from near 2.0 with a fixed bed to
near 4.0 with a mobile one, so the vertical structure of this system is not
separable from the sediment dynamics that shape it.
