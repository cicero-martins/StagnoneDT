\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

In a shallow Mediterranean lagoon, where the seagrass canopy occupies most of
the basin and the mean depth is below one metre, the canopy is the first-order
control on Lagrangian transport. Representing it raises Liu--Weisberg skill by
0.087 to 0.256 depending on the configuration it enters, with every bootstrap
interval clear of zero, against $-0.042$ to $+0.155$ for wave coupling and
$-0.071$ to $+0.119$ for bed mobility, neither of which holds its sign.

Where the drag is applied matters more than how it is calibrated. The canopy
here is a three-dimensional drag field parameterised entirely from published
measurement of the two species, with nothing fitted. It roughly doubles the
ratio of surface to bed speed and lowers bed shear stress by a factor of 14,
where representing the same meadow as an equivalent bed roughness raises that
stress. A treatment that changes the shape of the velocity profile relocates a
surface drifter, where a treatment changing only its magnitude does not.

Process attribution from a factorial is conditional on the factors the
factorial contains. In the four configurations carrying no canopy, wave coupling
and bed mobility interact with a term of $+0.189$ against an additive prediction
of $-0.105$. In the four carrying one, that term is $+0.028$ and both contrasts
lose their significance. The interaction describes the configurations that
produced it rather than the basin, and no statistic internal to those
configurations says so.

Where a water level gauge sits decides what it can constrain. Across the eight
configurations, anomaly RMSE differs by 2.1 and 9.2~mm at the two gauges near
the inlets, with no consistent ordering, and by 36.2~mm at the gauge inside the
vegetated basin, where the two arms separate without overlap and correlation
moves from 0.78--0.82 to 0.94--0.98. The same simulations differ by 0.27 in
Liu--Weisberg skill.
