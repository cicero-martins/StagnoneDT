\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All eight members are scored on a common interval, 8 to 10 July 2025, which is
the intersection of the members' post-spinup spans and which contains the whole
drifter record. Members are coded by wave coupling (W), seagrass canopy (V) and
mobile bed (M).

\subsection{Water level}
\label{sec:res_wl}

Table~\ref{tab:wl} reports water level skill at the three interior stations.
Raw error is dominated by a positive bias at BocaNord and AltaVilaEst, between
0.08 and 0.18~m, and is small at BocaSud, between $-0.021$ and $+0.026$~m. The
bias is a datum and boundary-offset property that all members share and it is
not what distinguishes them.

The anomaly RMSE behaves differently at the two outer stations and at the inner
one. At BocaSud it varies by 2.1~mm across the eight members and at BocaNord by
9.2~mm, in both cases without a consistent ordering. At AltaVilaEst it varies by
36.2~mm, and there the ordering is consistent and follows a single factor. The
four members carrying a canopy give 0.015 to 0.027~m with correlations of 0.94
to 0.98, the four without give 0.045 to 0.051~m with correlations of 0.78 to
0.82, and no member of one group overlaps the other. Modelled-to-observed
standard deviation ratios move the same way, from 1.13 to 1.33 without a canopy
to 0.98 to 1.17 with one.

AltaVilaEst is the innermost station, in the shallow northern basin where the
meadow is densest and the mean depth is smallest, so it is where a canopy would
be expected to matter most, while the two outer stations sit near the inlets
where the exchange is set by the boundary.

The vegetated members are three-day restart segments and the bare members are
continuous nine-day runs, so the separation at AltaVilaEst could in principle
reflect the restart rather than the canopy. A control member settles this. It
carries the same restart file, window and configuration as the vegetated
fixed-bed member with the vegetation removed, so it differs from the continuous
bare member only by being a segment. At AltaVilaEst it gives an anomaly RMSE of
0.051~m and a correlation of 0.82, against 0.051 and 0.82 for the continuous
bare member and 0.027 and 0.94 for the vegetated one. The two outer stations
agree with their continuous counterparts to within 0.4~mm. The restart accounts
for none of the difference.

\subsection{Lagrangian transport}
\label{sec:res_drifters}

Thirty-five drifters across twelve deployments were scored against each member
over the interval each drifter was in the water, which ranged from 0.4 to
7.2~hours. Table~\ref{tab:lagrangian} summarises the metrics and
Figure~\ref{fig:lagrangian} shows their distribution.

Mean Liu--Weisberg skill spans 0.469 to 0.735 across the eight members and
endpoint separation spans 345 to 618~m. The ensemble divides cleanly on one
factor. The four members carrying a canopy occupy 0.693 to 0.735 and the four
without occupy 0.469 to 0.624, and again the two groups do not overlap. Within
the vegetated group the standard deviation of skill across the four cells is
0.016, against 0.057 in the bare group, so once the canopy is represented the
choice of wave and bed treatment makes little difference to transport.

The two components of the trajectory error move together and both improve with
the canopy. Path length ratio rises from 0.73 to 0.84 without a canopy to 0.88
to 0.94 with one, and mean heading error falls from 11.3 to 19.8$^\circ$ to a
band of 8.3 to 9.0$^\circ$ that all four vegetated members share.

\subsection{Process attribution}
\label{sec:res_attribution}

The closed design supports twelve single-factor contrasts, four for each factor,
one for every combination of the other two. Figure~\ref{fig:attribution} gives
them with bootstrap confidence intervals over 4000 paired resamples.

\textbf{The canopy acts in every configuration.} It raises skill by 0.195 (95\%
CI $+0.110$ to $+0.278$) without waves on a fixed bed, 0.188 (CI $+0.111$ to
$+0.262$) with waves on a fixed bed, 0.256 (CI $+0.169$ to $+0.344$) without
waves on a mobile bed and 0.087 (CI $+0.054$ to $+0.124$) with waves on a mobile
bed. All four intervals are clear of zero, 26 to 32 of the 35 drifters improve
in each, and it is the only factor of the three whose four contrasts share a
sign. It is also the largest, by a factor of at least two over anything else in
the design.

\textbf{Wave coupling costs skill on a fixed bed and does nothing on a mobile
one.} On a fixed bed it reduces skill by 0.035 (CI $-0.052$ to $-0.017$) without
a canopy and 0.042 (CI $-0.057$ to $-0.027$) with one, consistently in both. On
a mobile bed the two arms disagree. Without a canopy it raises skill by 0.155
(CI $+0.059$ to $+0.247$), with 27 of 35 drifters better. With a canopy the same
contrast is $-0.014$, its interval crosses zero, and the drifters split 17
against 18.

\textbf{Bed mobility behaves the same way.} Without waves it reduces skill by
0.071 (CI $-0.085$ to $-0.057$) in the bare arm, degrading all 35 drifters, and
by 0.010 (CI $-0.014$ to $-0.006$) in the vegetated arm. With waves it raises
skill by 0.119 (CI $+0.045$ to $+0.191$) in the bare arm and by 0.018 in the
vegetated arm, where the interval crosses zero and the drifters split 16 against
19.

Stated as an interaction, waves and bed mobility each cost skill alone in the
bare arm, $-0.035$ and $-0.071$, and gain $+0.084$ together, so the interaction
term is $+0.189$ against an additive prediction of $-0.105$. In the vegetated
arm the same term is $+0.028$. The synergy between wave coupling and bed
mobility that the bare arm displays is not reproduced once the canopy is
present.

Deployment 4 is retained in all of the above and is the one deployment that
argues against the canopy, its bare members scoring 0.77 to 0.84 against 0.56 to
0.57 for its vegetated ones. It is also a sampling outlier, 0.4~hours in the
water over 162~m of observed path against next-lowest values of 1.9~hours and
723~m. Excluding it widens every canopy contrast, to $+0.240$, $+0.230$,
$+0.298$ and $+0.095$, and takes the wave contrast on a vegetated mobile bed to
$-0.002$. Because every difference it makes favours the conclusion, it is kept.

\subsection{Three-dimensional structure}
\label{sec:res_3d}

Over the wet cells of the lagoon interior, with the inlets excluded, mean
current speed increases monotonically from the bed to the surface in every
member (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}). The canopy sets the shape of that profile.

Without a canopy, bed-layer speed is 0.031 to 0.044~\si{\metre\per\second} and
surface-layer speed 0.067 to 0.083~\si{\metre\per\second}, for surface-to-bed
ratios of 1.85 to 2.14. With a canopy, bed-layer speed falls to a narrow band of
0.0096 to 0.0100~\si{\metre\per\second} while surface speed falls much less, to
0.043 to 0.052~\si{\metre\per\second}, and the ratio rises to 4.45 to 5.25.

The two other factors move the ratio by far less. Within the bare arm wave
coupling adds 0.22 on a fixed bed and 0.27 on a mobile one, and within the
vegetated arm 0.61 and 0.81. The canopy contribution, taken cell by cell, is
$+2.61$, $+3.00$, $+2.58$ and $+3.11$. The vertical structure of this basin is
therefore a canopy signature an order of magnitude larger than the wave
signature, and not the reverse.

Lagoon-mean surface speed does not order the members the way transport does. The
member reproducing the observed drifter behaviour best has a lagoon-mean surface
speed of 0.043~\si{\metre\per\second}, the lowest of the eight, against 0.083
for a member whose particles travel 27\% too slowly.

\subsection{Response of the seagrass classes}
\label{sec:res_classes}

Cells were grouped by the class assigned to them in the seagrass map, taking the
dominant class at the nearest classified point to each face centroid. Comparing
classes within a single member does not isolate the canopy, because the classes
occupy different parts of the basin at different depths. The bare members make
this explicit. With no canopy applied at all, their \emph{Posidonia} cells are
already slower at the bed than their sand cells, in a ratio of 0.84 to 0.94. The
canopy effect is therefore read between members within a class, or as a change
in that within-member ratio.

Read either way it is large and correctly ordered. Introducing the canopy
reduces mean bed speed by 80\% over \emph{Posidonia} and 80\% over
\emph{Cymodocea}, against 61\% over sand and 60\% over rock, averaged over the
four cells of the design (Figure~\ref{fig:classes}b). The \emph{Posidonia} to
sand bed-speed ratio falls from 0.84 to 0.94 without a canopy to 0.45 to 0.47
with one, in all four cells. The two unvegetated classes respond as well,
because slowing the flow through the meadow changes the circulation of a basin
whose interior is largely meadow, but they respond by a quarter less.

The wave field does not follow. Mean orbital velocity at the bed changes by
$-1.5$\% over \emph{Posidonia}, $-2.0$\% over \emph{Cymodocea}, $-2.3$\% over
sand and $-0.6$\% over rock when the canopy is introduced on a fixed bed
(Figure~\ref{fig:classes}c), which is neither large nor ordered by canopy
density. The reason is structural rather than numerical. In this coupling the
flow model passes a bed roughness field to the wave model, and the canopy is
represented in the momentum equation of the flow rather than in that roughness
field, so it never reaches the wave computation. The seagrass in these
simulations attenuates the current and not the waves, and Section~\ref{sec:disc_limits}
returns to what that leaves unresolved.
