\section{Results}

\subsection{Water level}
\label{sec:res_wl}

Table~\ref{tab:wl} reports water level skill at the three interior stations for
every ensemble member over the post-spinup window. Raw error is dominated by a
positive bias at BocaNord and AltaVilaEst, between 0.09 and 0.18~m, and is
small at BocaSud, between $-0.007$ and $+0.018$~m. Removing the respective
means leaves an anomaly RMSE of 0.021 to 0.045~m at every station in every
member, with correlations from 0.80 to 0.93 and modelled-to-observed standard
deviation ratios from 1.06 to 1.26.

The spread across configurations is small. Anomaly RMSE varies by 0.006~m or
less between members at any given station, and the ordering of members is not
consistent from one station to another.

\subsection{Lagrangian transport}
\label{sec:res_drifters}

Thirty-five drifters across twelve deployments were scored against each member
over the interval each drifter was in the water, which ranged from 0.5 to
7.2~hours. Table~\ref{tab:lagrangian} summarises the metrics and
Figure~\ref{fig:lagrangian} shows their distribution.

Mean Liu--Weisberg skill was 0.539 and 0.548 for the two members without wave
coupling, 0.505 and 0.506 for their wave-coupled fixed-bed counterparts, 0.524
with a mobile bed under uniform roughness and 0.655 with a mobile bed under
distributed roughness. Endpoint separation ranges from 549 to 661~m across the
first five members and falls to 334~m in the last. At drifter level the
distributions overlap heavily (Figure~\ref{fig:lagrangian}a), so the ordering
of the means rests on paired comparison rather than on separation between
groups.

The wave penalty is neither carried by a few deployments nor dependent on the
roughness treatment (Figure~\ref{fig:lagrangian}b). Under uniform roughness,
skill falls with wave coupling in 28 of the 35 drifters and in 10 of the 12
deployments (sign test $p = 0.0005$). Under distributed roughness the
corresponding counts are 29 of 35 and 10 of 12 ($p = 0.0001$). The two
deployments in which waves help are the same two in both treatments, and all
three of their drifters improve in each, so they represent a genuine difference
in behaviour rather than scatter.

The observed drifters travelled at a mean speed of 0.143~\si{\metre\per\second},
and only the member combining a mobile bed with distributed roughness
reproduces that rate, at 0.97 of the observed path length. The four fixed-bed
members fall 19 to 22\% short. A mobile bed under uniform roughness recovers
most of the path length, at 0.93, while losing accuracy in direction, which is
why its endpoint separation is the worst of the six.

Figure~\ref{fig:tracks} shows the tracks these statistics are computed from,
for four deployments spanning the duration range. The two failure modes are
visible directly. The fixed-bed member stops short of the observed endpoint in
every panel. The mobile-bed member under uniform roughness covers a comparable
distance to the observed drifter but in the wrong direction, most clearly in
deployments 3 and 10, where it leaves the observed track early and ends farther
from the observed endpoint than the fixed-bed member does despite travelling
farther. Only the member combining a mobile bed with distributed roughness
follows the observed path in both respects.

\subsection{Process attribution}
\label{sec:res_attribution}

Figure~\ref{fig:attribution} gives the paired per-drifter contrasts with
bootstrap confidence intervals.

Wave coupling reduces Liu--Weisberg skill on both roughness axes, by 0.035
under uniform roughness (95\% CI $-0.052$ to $-0.017$, $p < 0.001$) and by
0.042 under distributed roughness (CI $-0.064$ to $-0.023$, $p < 0.001$). The
two estimates are statistically indistinguishable from one another, so the wave
penalty does not depend on how vegetation resistance is represented.

Distributed roughness on a fixed bed changes skill by 0.001 with wave coupling
(CI $-0.016$ to $+0.017$, $p = 0.35$) and by 0.009 without it (CI $+0.004$ to
$+0.013$, $p = 0.004$). Both are negligible beside the other contrasts. A
mobile bed under uniform roughness changes skill by 0.019 (CI $-0.071$ to
$+0.108$, $p = 0.66$).

The two contrasts in which both treatments are present are of a different order.
Distributed roughness on a mobile bed gives $+0.131$ (CI $+0.092$ to $+0.175$,
$p < 0.001$), improving 32 of the 35 drifters. A mobile bed under distributed
roughness gives $+0.149$ (CI $+0.052$ to $+0.245$, $p = 0.006$).

Neither treatment produces more than a marginal change on its own, and their
joint effect exceeds the sum of the individual point estimates by an order of
magnitude, so the two interact rather than contribute additively
(Figure~\ref{fig:attribution}d).

\subsection{Three-dimensional structure}
\label{sec:res_3d}

Over 4043 wet cells in the lagoon interior, with the inlets excluded, mean
current speed increases monotonically from the bed to the surface in every
member (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}). Bed-layer speed ranges from 0.021 to
0.040~\si{\metre\per\second} and surface-layer speed from 0.075 to
0.100~\si{\metre\per\second}, giving surface-to-bed ratios between 1.85 and
4.42.

The fixed-bed members give nearly identical profiles, with ratios between 1.85
and 2.07. Both mobile-bed members roughly double the ratio, to 4.42 and 3.77,
and they do so through the bed layer rather than the surface, with near-bed
speed falling from 0.038 to 0.021~\si{\metre\per\second} while surface speed
changes by less. Adding distributed roughness to a mobile bed lowers surface
speed from 0.100 to 0.078~\si{\metre\per\second}.

Lagoon-mean surface speed does not order the members the way transport does.
The member reproducing the observed drifter speed has a lagoon-mean surface
speed of 0.078~\si{\metre\per\second}, below the 0.080 of a member whose
particles travel 20\% too slowly.

\subsection{Response of the roughness classes}
\label{sec:res_classes}

Cells were grouped by the trachytope class the model assigned them, taking the
dominant class at the nearest roughness link. Comparing classes within a single
member does not isolate the roughness treatment, because the classes occupy
different parts of the basin at different depths. The uniform-roughness members
make this explicit. With no roughness distinction applied at all, their
\emph{Posidonia} cells are still slower at the bed than their sand cells, in a
ratio of 0.86 and 0.87. The roughness effect is therefore read between members
within a class rather than between classes within a member.

On a fixed bed, moving from uniform to distributed roughness reduces mean bed
speed by 1.3\% over sand, 1.1\% over \emph{Cymodocea}, 3.6\% over
\emph{Posidonia} and 4.5\% over rock (Figure~\ref{fig:classes}b). The effect on
the wave field is larger and ordered as canopy attenuation predicts. Mean
orbital velocity at the bed falls by 11.3\% over sand, 13.7\% over
\emph{Cymodocea} and 14.3\% over \emph{Posidonia}, and by only 3.3\% over rock
(Figure~\ref{fig:classes}c), the rock class being the exposed substrate near
the inlets where mean orbital velocity reaches 0.27~\si{\metre\per\second}
against 0.05 to 0.09~\si{\metre\per\second} elsewhere in the basin.

Introducing bed mobility inverts the class ordering of near-bed speed. The
\emph{Posidonia} to sand ratio rises from 0.85 on a fixed bed to 1.09 and 1.28
in the two mobile-bed members, because sand cells lose roughly half their
near-bed speed while \emph{Posidonia} cells lose about a third.
