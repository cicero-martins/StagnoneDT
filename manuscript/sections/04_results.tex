\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Water level}
\label{sec:res_wl}

Table~\ref{tab:wl} reports water level skill at the three interior stations for
every ensemble member over the post-spinup window. Raw error is dominated by a
positive bias at BocaNord and AltaVilaEst, between 0.10 and 0.18~m, and is small
at BocaSud, between $-0.007$ and $+0.018$~m. Removing the respective means
leaves an anomaly RMSE of 0.021 to 0.042~m at every station in every member,
with correlations from 0.79 to 0.93 and modelled-to-observed standard deviation
ratios from 1.06 to 1.16.

The spread across configurations is smaller than the spread within a tidal
cycle. Anomaly RMSE varies by 4.2~mm at BocaNord, 2.7~mm at AltaVilaEst and
1.9~mm at BocaSud between the eight members, and the ordering of members is not
consistent from one station to another.

\subsection{Lagrangian transport}
\label{sec:res_drifters}

Thirty-five drifters across twelve deployments were scored against each member
over the interval each drifter was in the water, which ranged from 0.5 to
7.2~hours. Table~\ref{tab:lagrangian} summarises the metrics and
Figure~\ref{fig:lagrangian} shows their distribution.

Mean Liu--Weisberg skill spans 0.469 to 0.705 across the eight members, a range
of 50\% of the smallest value, and endpoint separation spans 336 to 625~m. The
ordering is not what a reading of the individual treatments would predict. The
two worst members are those combining a mobile bed with no wave coupling, at
0.469 and 0.473, below all four fixed-bed members. The two best are those
combining a mobile bed with wave coupling, at 0.624 and 0.705. The same two
treatments therefore produce the worst and the best members of the ensemble
depending only on whether they are applied together.

\subsection{Process attribution}
\label{sec:res_attribution}

The closed design supports twelve single-factor contrasts, four for each factor,
one for every combination of the other two. Figure~\ref{fig:attribution} gives
them with bootstrap confidence intervals.

\textbf{Wave coupling changes sign with the bed.} On a fixed bed it reduces
skill, by 0.035 under uniform roughness (95\% CI $-0.052$ to $-0.017$) and by
0.042 under distributed roughness (CI $-0.064$ to $-0.023$), with 28 and 29 of
the 35 drifters worse and 10 of the 12 deployments worse in each. On a mobile
bed it raises skill, by 0.155 (CI $+0.059$ to $+0.247$) and by 0.232 (CI
$+0.131$ to $+0.332$), with 27 and 30 drifters better and only 2 of 12
deployments worse in each. All four intervals are clear of zero.

\textbf{Bed mobility changes sign with the waves.} Without wave coupling it
reduces skill by 0.071 (CI $-0.085$ to $-0.057$) and 0.075 (CI $-0.092$ to
$-0.061$), and it does so for every drifter: 35 of 35 worse in both roughness
treatments. With wave coupling it raises skill by 0.119 (CI $+0.045$ to
$+0.191$) and 0.199 (CI $+0.111$ to $+0.284$).

\textbf{Distributed roughness acts only when both are present.} It changes skill
by 0.009 on a fixed bed without waves, 0.001 on a fixed bed with waves, and
0.004 on a mobile bed without waves, none of which is material and two of which
have intervals crossing zero. On a mobile bed with wave coupling it gives
$+0.081$ (CI $+0.055$ to $+0.113$), improving 34 of the 35 drifters.

Neither wave coupling nor bed mobility improves prediction on its own, and each
is what makes the other work. Applied singly they are the two treatments that
make the model worse.

\subsection{Three-dimensional structure}
\label{sec:res_3d}

Over 4043 wet cells in the lagoon interior, with the inlets excluded, mean
current speed increases monotonically from the bed to the surface in every
member (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}). Bed-layer speed ranges from 0.026 to
0.044~\si{\metre\per\second} and surface-layer speed from 0.056 to
0.083~\si{\metre\per\second}, giving surface-to-bed ratios between 1.85 and
2.20.

The ratio is set by wave coupling rather than by bed mobility. The four members
without waves lie between 1.85 and 1.87 regardless of their bed treatment, and
the four with waves between 2.05 and 2.20. Adding a mobile bed to a wave-coupled
member raises the ratio by 0.07 to 0.15, an order of magnitude less than the
wave contribution.

Lagoon-mean surface speed does not order the members the way transport does. The
member reproducing the observed drifter speed has the lowest lagoon-mean surface
speed of the eight, at 0.056~\si{\metre\per\second}, below the 0.083 of a member
whose particles travel 27\% too slowly.

\subsection{Response of the roughness classes}
\label{sec:res_classes}

Cells were grouped by the trachytope class the model assigned them, taking the
dominant class at the nearest roughness link. Comparing classes within a single
member does not isolate the roughness treatment, because the classes occupy
different parts of the basin at different depths. The uniform-roughness members
make this explicit. With no roughness distinction applied at all, their
\emph{Posidonia} cells are still slower at the bed than their sand cells, in a
ratio of 0.85 to 0.87. The roughness effect is therefore read between members
within a class rather than between classes within a member.

On a fixed bed, moving from uniform to distributed roughness reduces mean bed
speed by 1.3\% over sand, 1.1\% over \emph{Cymodocea}, 3.6\% over
\emph{Posidonia} and 4.5\% over rock (Figure~\ref{fig:classes}b). The effect on
the wave field is larger and ordered as canopy attenuation predicts. Mean
orbital velocity at the bed falls by 11.3\% over sand, 13.7\% over
\emph{Cymodocea} and 14.3\% over \emph{Posidonia}, and by only 3.3\% over rock
(Figure~\ref{fig:classes}c), the rock class being the exposed substrate near the
inlets where mean orbital velocity reaches 0.27~\si{\metre\per\second} against
0.05 to 0.09~\si{\metre\per\second} elsewhere in the basin.

The canopy parametrisation therefore does to the wave field and to the near-bed
layer what a canopy should, on a fixed bed, where it changes surface transport
by an amount indistinguishable from zero.
